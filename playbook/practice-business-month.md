# Practice business month · follow the money

Original teaching example, November 18, 2026. Every number is invented for practice. The example uses a simple accrual management view and a sole-owner capital/withdrawal presentation. It does not select a legal entity or tax accounting method. Tax, employees, payment processing, debt and equipment are absent from these example transactions.

## Before you look at the answers

You contribute $150 to the business. It buys $60 of supplies for cash, delivers one $180 order using $45 of those supplies, and pays a $25 operating fee. The customer has been invoiced but has not paid. You worked six hours and choose $20/hour as the value of your time.

Answer these questions.

1. How much cash remains?
2. How much did the order earn before owner-time valuation and tax?
3. What supplies remain and what is the customer balance?
4. Can the business pay a $100 cash bill tomorrow if the customer pays later?
5. When the customer pays, does that create another $180 sale?
6. If you then withdraw $40, does it change the order's profit?
7. What invoice would cover the existing costs and your chosen time value?

## Transaction record

An increase is positive and a decrease negative. Sales and profit are recognized when the order has been delivered in this simplified example. The running columns show balances after each event.

| Event | Cash movement | Cash balance | Customer owes | Supplies left | Cumulative profit | Owner's equity |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Start | $0 | $0 | $0 | $0 | $0 | $0 |
| Owner contributes money | +$150 | $150 | $0 | $0 | $0 | $150 |
| Buy supplies | −$60 | $90 | $0 | $60 | $0 | $150 |
| Deliver and invoice order, using $45 of supplies | $0 | $90 | $180 | $15 | $135 | $285 |
| Pay operating fee | −$25 | $65 | $180 | $15 | $110 | $260 |
| Customer pays later | +$180 | $245 | $0 | $15 | $110 | $260 |
| Owner withdraws cash | −$40 | $205 | $0 | $15 | $110 | $220 |

Each row balances. Assets are cash + customer balance + remaining supplies, liabilities are zero, and equity equals cumulative owner contributions + profit − withdrawals. A supply purchase changes the form of an asset. Consumption of the supplies becomes a cost of the delivered order.

## Four views before collection

### Profit and loss for the period

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue earned | $180 |
| Supplies used, cost of goods sold | −$45 |
| Gross profit | $135 |
| Operating fee | −$25 |
| Profit before income tax and owner-time valuation | **$110** |

### Cash flows from opening through the unpaid invoice

| Cash activity | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Customer cash receipts | $0 |
| Supply payments | −$60 |
| Operating fee payment | −$25 |
| Net operating cash movement | **−$85** |
| Owner funding | +$150 |
| Closing cash | **$65** |

A $100 bill due before customer payment creates a $35 shortfall. A different due date, customer deposit, smaller supply purchase or additional funding could change the timing. Evaluate the actual option and terms before agreeing.

### Balance sheet at the same time

| Assets | Amount | Liabilities and equity | Amount |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Cash | $65 | Liabilities | $0 |
| Customer balance, accounts receivable | $180 | Owner's contributed capital | $150 |
| Supplies inventory | $15 | Profit retained | $110 |
| Total | **$260** | Total | **$260** |

### Change in owner's equity

Opening equity $0 + contributions $150 + profit $110 − withdrawals $0 = **$260**. After the later $40 withdrawal, equity is **$220**. The later collection changes cash and receivables, while profit and equity stay unchanged.

## The operator's decision

Profit per total work hour is $110 / 6 = **$18.33**, before income tax and other missing costs. Valuing six hours at $20 uses $120. Economic surplus after that owner-time value is $110 − $120 = **−$10**.

A $190 invoice would cover $45 consumed supplies + $25 operating fee + $120 chosen time value under these assumptions. A seventh hour raises that threshold to $210. A $20 extra contribution cushion raises the six-hour threshold to $210. Real payment fees, refunds, acquisition work or added resources would change it.

The extra $15 of unused supplies still ties up cash. It could support a later order if it remains usable. If all $15 becomes unusable and is written off, profit falls to $95 and the six-hour return to $15.83/hour. Reprice the next order or reduce its full cost/time based on the changed facts.

## Final cash-flow tie-out after collection and withdrawal

Operating cash flow is $180 collected − $60 supplies purchased − $25 fee = **$95**. Net owner funding is $150 contributed − $40 withdrawn = **$110**. Together they explain the $205 closing cash balance. The $15 still held as inventory explains why operating cash flow is $15 below the $110 profit. If that remaining inventory spoils, cash stays $205, profit falls to $95 and equity falls to $205.

The $70 of consumed supplies and operating fees is the accounting-cost break-even price in this simplified one-order example. The $190 price adds the chosen six-hour owner compensation, and $210 adds a seventh hour or the specified cushion. Those are economic price thresholds under the stated assumptions.

## Teach-back answers

Cash is $65 before collection. Profit is $110. Supplies are $15 and receivables $180. Collection creates no additional revenue in this example. After collection and a $40 owner withdrawal, cash is $205, supplies $15, assets/equity $220 and profit remains $110.

Explain those answers in your own words before applying the [operating review](operating-review-template.md) to an idea. Extend the exercise with a late payment, discarded stock or an extra work hour, and record the decision each change creates.
