## New ranked objections (most damaging first)

### 1. The 30 to 60 bowls per morning number has no source, and it is a conversion rate in disguise
Where: validation-backlog.md working hypothesis; pilot-and-equipment.md Stage 1 and 2.
The archive never says how many people work at the campus it imagines. The target only means something as a share of headcount. As arithmetic only (the headcounts are placeholders, not data): 30 bowls is 10 percent of a 300-person campus every single day, and 30 percent of a 100-person one. 60 bowls is 20 percent of 300 and 12 percent of 500. The question is whether that share of office workers buy a hot breakfast from a stand at the door, at $6 to $8, on an ordinary Tuesday.
Why it could hurt: if the realistic share is a fraction of that, the pilot works on novelty week and fades. The archive's own gate asks for repeat demand "on multiple weeks," which is right, but the sizing behind the target is missing.
Cheap test, zero spend: stand across from a candidate building entrance from 6:30 to 9:30 on two weekdays and count people entering, and how many carry coffee or food. Then ask 10 people who work in offices (friends of friends is fine) what they ate for breakfast this week, where they got it, what it cost, and whether they would buy oatmeal at the door at $5.95 to $7.95 and how often. Settles it: a real headcount-times-share estimate to replace the 30 to 60 guess.
Not researched anywhere in the archive: what nearby coffee shops, free office snacks and breakfast habits already compete for that same morning dollar. I have not researched it either.

### 2. The money model stops at gross sales, and at Stage 1 the gross is small
Where: pilot-and-equipment.md table. I rechecked the arithmetic (bowls per day times ticket times days per week times 4.33) and it is correct, except for a two-dollar slip in the Stage 3 low end, which should read $13,369, not $13,367.
Stage 1 gross is $1,949 to $3,410 per month, which is about $450 to $790 per week for three mornings, before ingredients, packaging, payment fees, commissary, permits, insurance, any host fee, fuel and the operator's time. The archive says all of that is excluded. It does not yet estimate any of them, so nobody can say what Charles would keep per hour.
Cheap test: one page. Put a cost on one bowl from a grocery receipt (oats, toppings, cup, lid, spoon, napkin). Then list weekly hours (prep, travel, setup, 3 hours of service, teardown, cleaning). Margin per bowl times bowls per week, minus fixed costs, divided by hours is the number that matters. Settles it: whether Stage 1 is a living, a side income, or a learning exercise. Charles should decide which of those he wants it to be before more research is done.

### 3. The average ticket in the model is higher than the menu can produce
Where: pilot-and-equipment.md. The starter menu is $5.95, $7.50 and $7.95. Add-ons are +$1.50 to +$2.00 for nuts and extras and $2 to $3 for a drink. The Stage 1 model uses a $7.50 average, Stage 2 uses $8, Stage 3 uses $9.50, and the three-hour sensitivity table uses $9.00 and $10.50.
Why it could hurt: an average above $7.95 needs most buyers to add something. If everyone buys the $5.95 Classic with no add-on, Stage 1 gross falls from the $1,949 to $3,410 range to roughly $1,546 to $2,705 per month (same formula at $5.95). The document also lists whether $5.95 to $7.95 is viable at all as an open question.
Cheap test: record what real buyers choose in the first tastings, and ask the "too cheap, bargain, getting expensive, too expensive" price questions. Settles it: a real average ticket.

### 4. Where the oatmeal gets cooked is not in the first-priority list, and it gates everything
Where: local-regulation.md says Charles should not assume he can cook at home, that many soup kettles only hold already-hot food, and that the prep site must be approved. But the P0 rows in validation-backlog.md are about host, parcel, and asking Central District Health to classify the setup. A licensed prep kitchen or commissary is not a P0 row and is not a cost line anywhere.
Why it could hurt: if there is no affordable approved kitchen, the plan has no legal way to make the product, and the rent for one is a fixed cost that changes the math in objection 2.
Cheap test: add "find and price an approved prep kitchen" to P0 and ask Central District Health the archive's own question 2 (can externally cooked oatmeal be transported to the warmers). Settles it: yes or no, plus a monthly cost.

### 5. The timeline to a legal first sale is longer than the plan implies, and one test step may be a problem
Where: local-regulation.md says plan review should be submitted at least 30 days in advance, with notice at least 15 days before opening. Per the Central District Health page and operational plan (confirmed by Well Actually), plan review must be submitted at least 30 days before opening, and the 15-day inspection notice runs after plan approval rather than stacking on top of the 30. So the earliest legal first sale is roughly 30 days or more from a complete submission, which means mid December at the earliest if work started today, and likely later.
Separately, validation-backlog.md P1 suggests an "actual refundable preorder invitation" and "permitted morning service test." Whether taking preorders or money before the food license exists is allowed is not answered anywhere. This is a question, not a claim. It belongs with Central District Health and the Back Office bot before anyone is asked to pay.
Cheap test: ask Central District Health whether a one-day trial needs the full license, and whether a free sampling day differs from a paid sale. Settles it: what can be tested before the 30-day wait.

### 6. One operator, one three-hour window, one arrival wave
Where: pilot-and-equipment.md says 60 bowls in three hours is one bowl every three minutes on average, and itself warns that office arrivals bunch up.
My guess, labeled as a guess: if half of the 60 bowls are wanted in the busiest hour, that is one bowl every two minutes while the same person takes payment, assembles toppings, keeps hands clean, and watches the warmers.
Cheap test: at home, time yourself making 20 bowls in a row from preportioned toppings with a friend playing the line and paying by phone. Settles it: the real seconds per bowl, which sets the maximum sensible line.

### 7. The public site and the poll now describe a different business than the pilot
Where: index.html still sells six bowls including Cheddar & Egg, Southwest and Mushroom & Herb, says "Breakfast & Lunch," and the live poll still asks about Apple Pie, Cheddar & Egg and Mushroom & Herb. The pilot menu is Classic, Maple Walnut and Apple Pie, with no egg and no savory. Maple Walnut is not on the site at all. The footer still says "Boise-area market research" (round 1 objection 6).
Why it could hurt: anyone Charles sends to the site, including a building manager, sees a different business than the one he is pitching, and the poll cannot say anything about the pilot menu.
Cheap fix (builder's files, so I have not touched them): when Charles decides, align the homepage and the poll to the pilot menu, and reword the footer. Until then, do not use the current poll result as evidence for the pilot.

### 8. The archive's own acquisition leads are closed oatmeal cafes, and nobody has asked why
Where: brand-and-marketing.md lists OatMeals (New York) and Oat Shop (Somerville) as "former" shops and possible acquisition leads.
What I found (outside sources, checked today): OatMeals' own site says it closed on January 24, 2021 because of the business impact of Covid-19 ([Our Story](https://www.oatmealsny.com/our-story-index)), after about 8.5 years. A Boston restaurant news item quotes Oat Shop saying it closed in July 2022 after about five and a half years citing rising costs and a lease ending ([Boston Restaurant Talk](https://bostonrestaurants.blogspot.com/2022/07/shirley-to-open-brick-and-mortar-space.html)).
How to read this honestly: it is a mixed signal. Both lasted for years, so oatmeal-led shops can survive in a dense city. But both were storefronts, OatMeals sold nearly 30 bowls plus pastries, neither was a one-person office stand, and both ended partly from cost pressure, which is exactly the number this plan has not modeled (objection 2). Worth a short note in the archive, not a reason to stop.

### 9. Dependence on a single host and on winter
Where: local-regulation.md risk register rates winter exposure and loss of host access as medium. One host means one landlord decision ends the business, and the plan needs a morning outdoor or semi-outdoor stand through Boise's cold months (general knowledge, not a measured fact). I would rate it higher than medium until a host has agreed in writing and a covered or indoor location is considered.
Cheap test: in the host interview, ask whether an indoor lobby or covered entry is possible. That changes which regulations apply, so ask Central District Health too.
