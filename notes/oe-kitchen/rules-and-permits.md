# Rules and permits: Idaho food law, CDH, City of Boise, ACHD, tax

**Date checked:** November 11, 2026 (America/Boise).  
**Not a legal opinion.** Fees, forms, and interpretations change. Confirm with the agency that will issue or deny the permit. For the one-day trial path, start with [one-day-trial-checklist.md](one-day-trial-checklist.md).

## 1. Idaho Direct-to-Consumer Commerce Act (former cottage food)

Senate Bill 1283 created Idaho Code Title 37, Chapter 2, effective **March 20, 2026**. The Idaho Department of Health and Welfare says the Act "replaces and supersedes the cottage food rule that was defined previously in IDAPA 16.02.19." Sources: [DHW food safety](https://healthandwelfare.idaho.gov/health-wellness/community-health/food-safety), [Chapter 2 index](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title37/t37ch2/), bill text at [S1283](https://legislature.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/sessioninfo/2026/legislation/S1283.pdf), checked November 11, 2026.

Central District Health points people who want to prepare food in a home kitchen and sell it directly to consumers to the DHW page, and to the Idaho Farm Bureau's "A Practical Guide to Idaho's Direct-to-Consumer Food Law." Sources: [CDH food establishments](https://cdh.idaho.gov/licenses-permits-inspections/food-establishments/), [Farm Bureau pamphlet (PDF)](https://www.idahofb.org/media/mi1burxb/direct-to-consumer-pamphlet.pdf), checked November 11, 2026. The pamphlet is informational, not legal advice, and not an agency order.

### What the statute actually says (plain language)

- **Homemade** means food or nonalcoholic drinks grown, prepared, or processed at a private or leased farm, home kitchen, or other non-licensed facility. **Homemade does not include** food that is prepared, cooked, or served on-site for immediate consumption, including restaurant-style food service, mobile food units, concession trailers, or other facilities preparing ready-to-eat meals. Source: [Idaho Code 37-203(5)](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title37/t37ch2/sect37-203/).
- Producers may sell homemade **shelf-stable or perishable** food to an informed end consumer, entirely inside Idaho. The buyer must be the final purchaser and may not resell or redistribute the product. Wholesale and out-of-state sales are not exempted by this Act. Sources: [37-205](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title37/t37ch2/sect37-205/), DHW food safety page.
- **Perishable food** means homemade food and drinks that require time or temperature control at 41°F or below, or 135°F or above. The statute's example list is "includes but is not limited to" and does not name oatmeal. Cooked oatmeal that must be kept hot or cold would, as a category, look like perishable / TCS food. Whether a specific recipe qualifies, and whether a given sale is "homemade" or "on-site immediate consumption," is for CDH or DHW to say. Source: [37-203(7)](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title37/t37ch2/sect37-203/).
- Required consumer notice, by sign, label, or card: "This product is not subject to government food safety inspection or licensing requirements. It may contain allergens." Plus the producer's name and contact information, and an ingredient list if the product has two or more ingredients. Perishable foods must also include safe-handling instructions. Source: [37-205(3)-(4)](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title37/t37ch2/sect37-205/).
- Keep transaction records at least two years: type and quantity sold, date of sale, date of production, where the food was produced, and where each ingredient was produced or acquired. No routine reporting to a health district, except in a confirmed foodborne-illness investigation. Fine of up to $500 if required records are missing during such an investigation. Source: [37-208](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title37/t37ch2/sect37-208/).
- Producers and designated agents must become familiar with educational material DHW is required to publish. Source: [37-206](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title37/t37ch2/sect37-206/).
- The Act occupies this field. Local governments and health districts may not impose stricter licensing, permitting, inspection, packaging, or labeling rules on products sold pursuant to the chapter. It does not limit liability for unsafe food, and it does not block a foodborne-illness investigation. Dairy and raw-milk rules still apply. A product sold under this chapter may not be used as an ingredient in a licensed food establishment. Source: [37-204](https://legislature.idaho.gov/statutesrules/idstat/title37/t37ch2/sect37-204/).

The Farm Bureau pamphlet, in its "What This Law Does NOT Allow" list, includes restaurants and food trucks, retail grocery, interstate sales, and resales. That matches the homemade definition's exclusion of on-site immediate consumption. Source: pamphlet PDF, checked November 11, 2026.

### What this means for Oats, Etc. products

| Product and sales method | Working reading (must be confirmed) |
|---|---|
| Hot oatmeal scooped into a bowl at a stand, cart, office counter, or event booth, for someone to eat now | Not homemade under 37-203(5). Expect a licensed food-establishment path (temporary, mobile, catering, or permanent), and usually approved prep, not a home kitchen. |
| Employer-paid or preorder drop-off of hot bowls, handed out as a meal | Likely still food-establishment / catering territory, not the homemade Act. UNVERIFIED. Ask CDH. |
| Packaged refrigerated overnight oats, sold in Idaho only, to the end consumer, with the required notice, handling instructions, and records, not served as a restaurant meal | Closer to the Act's perishable homemade model. Still **UNVERIFIED for this recipe**. Ask CDH and DHW. City of Boise exempts qualifying Act sales from the City Food and Beverage license ([Code 3-5-6(B)(7)](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/boise_id/latest/boise/0-0-0-88558)), but zoning, home occupation, tax, and market rules can still apply. |
| Shelf-stable dry oat mixes or granola sold direct in Idaho | More clearly in the older cottage-food tradition and in the Act's shelf-stable examples (granola is listed). Still confirm labeling and records. This is not the current pilot hero product. |
| Selling the same homemade product to a café or grocer for resale, or shipping out of state | Not covered by the Act. That is when a licensed kitchen and other approvals typically become mandatory. |

**Do not treat "Idaho food freedom" as a green light for the office breakfast stand.** That is the mistake this research is meant to prevent. Ask CDH the script in the one-day trial checklist.

Older IDAPA cottage-food materials (non-TCS baked goods only) and some district FAQs written before March 20, 2026, are outdated if they say perishable foods can never be sold from home. The 2026 Act is broader than that, and also narrower than a blanket "cook anything at home and serve it like a restaurant."

## 2. When a licensed kitchen is required

CDH's current page says establishments that offer Time/Temperature Controlled for Safety (TCS) foods must be licensed and regulated. That list includes restaurants, mobile trucks and carts, temporary and catered events, concession stands, and food processors. To become a regulated establishment you apply, complete plan review, receive a pre-operational inspection, and then receive a license. Source: [CDH food establishments](https://cdh.idaho.gov/licenses-permits-inspections/food-establishments/), checked November 11, 2026.

CDH's December 28, 2023 farmers-market handout says potentially hazardous food must be prepared in an approved facility or on site, and may not be prepared in a home kitchen. That handout predates the Act and still lists a $72 multiple-day fee (the current CDH webpage lists $80). Treat it as possibly stale on fees and on how it interacts with the 2026 Act, and confirm with CDH. Source: [Farmers Market Information (PDF)](https://cdh.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Farmers-Market-Information.pdf).

The Commons' own site states a related practical point: a home kitchen can never be licensed as a commercial food establishment in Idaho, so wholesale or out-of-state growth needs a licensed facility. That is marketing copy, not a statute, but it matches the ordinary food-establishment framework. Confirm with CDH. Source: [thecommonskitchen.com](https://www.thecommonskitchen.com/), checked November 11, 2026.

Mobile units that are not self-sufficient generally need a commissary / servicing area, a commissary agreement, and daily servicing. Plan review must be submitted at least 30 days before operation, with approval before construction or remodel, and CDH notified at least 15 days before opening for a pre-operational inspection. Hot holding is described as 135°F or above, cold holding 41°F or below. Source: [Mobile Food Unit Operational Plan (PDF, rev. 12-23)](https://cdh.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Mobile-Food-Operational-Plan.pdf), checked November 11, 2026.

## 3. Temporary food events, farmers markets, and the "one-day trial"

Covered in detail in [one-day-trial-checklist.md](one-day-trial-checklist.md). Short restatement with sources:

- TFE: booth or stand, no more than 14 consecutive days, in conjunction with a single event or celebration. Fees: $35 / $45 / $80. Apply at least 30 days ahead. License issued at the event after a passing inspection, per CDH's temporary-event handout. The fee covers the temporary or intermittent establishment for the calendar year under the conditions in that handout (menu changes and added events can require a new application or fee). Sources: CDH webpage and [Temporary Food Establishment Information (PDF)](https://cdh.idaho.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Temporary-Food-Establishment-Information.pdf).
- A restaurant or caterer's annual license does not automatically cover a TFE at a public event. Public events are not considered catered events, per that same handout.
- Whether a private office morning is a TFE "event" is **UNVERIFIED**.
- Capital City Public Market additionally requires a commercial kitchen for specialty food vendors (a market rule, which may be stricter than the 2026 Act for packaged homemade food), $1 million general liability insurance, a Temporary Food Establishment Permit, tax registration, a $35 application fee, and a 10-by-10 white canopy with 20-pound weights. The apply page had conflicting "applications closed" and "applications are open for the holiday season" text on November 11, 2026. Confirm with (208) 345-3499 or ccpmarket@gmail.com. Source: [capitalcitypublicmarket.com/apply](https://www.capitalcitypublicmarket.com/apply).

## 6. City of Boise licensing and zoning

Boise does not use one universal "business license" for every company. It licenses named categories, including Food and Beverage and Vendor / Temporary Merchant / Solicitor. Sources: [Food and Beverage License](https://www.cityofboise.org/departments/city-clerk/licensing/food-and-alcohol-beverage/food-and-beverage-license/), [Vendor License](https://www.cityofboise.org/departments/city-clerk/licensing/other/vendor-license/), [Licensing Fee Schedule](https://www.cityofboise.org/departments/city-clerk/licensing-fee-schedule/), [Boise City Code Title 3, Chapter 5](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/boise_id/latest/boise/0-0-0-88558) (Ord. 29-26, June 30, 2026), all checked November 11, 2026.

Fees published November 11, 2026, plus a $3.00 processing fee listed separately: Food and Beverage $84.00; Vendor annual $148.00, semi-annual $74.00; Temporary Merchant $69.75. Vendor and temporary-merchant applications may need a $1,000 (person) or $5,000 (company) cash or surety bond. Temporary merchants and solicitors may need in-person fingerprinting at City Hall. Applications require a current Idaho Secretary of State registration matching the license name. If CDH suspends or revokes its license, the City food-and-beverage license is also suspended or revoked.

Code 3-5-6(B)(7) exempts qualifying Direct-to-Consumer Act sales from the City Food and Beverage Establishment License. That is not a blanket exemption from vendor, zoning, or tax rules.

The City definition of a mobile food and beverage establishment includes portable tables and containers used for retail sale at temporary locations (Code 3-5-5). Ask the Clerk how they classify your stand.

Public sidewalk and street vending is a different, tighter box (Title 3, Chapter 2), including downtown medallions, approved carts, hours, and limits on public-way utility connections. The 2026 vendor amendments (Ordinance 29-26) affect hours in some commercially zoned areas. Confirm the current text with the Clerk for your address. Code publication: [vendor restrictions 3-2-12](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/boise_id/latest/boise/0-0-0-1673) and the [2026 ordinance text](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/boise_id/latest/boise/0-0-0-88829). The online code may lag. Believe the Clerk.

For a private commercial lot, Planning must classify the use. Boise zoning has accessory food-truck standards and temporary mobile food-truck provisions (including, in published zoning materials, limits such as 7 consecutive days and a cap on occupying required parking). A small portable counter may or may not be treated like a "food truck." That is a Planning question. Zoning code PDF cited in the project research archive: [2025 Boise Zoning Code](https://www.cityofboise.org/media/20188/2025-boise-zoning-code-effective-july-1-2025.pdf). Confirm the current version with Planning at (208) 608-7100 or zoninginfo@cityofboise.org.

Home occupation: if you run a business from a Boise home (office work, storage, or any production CDH might allow), ask Planning about a zoning compliance / home-occupation review. Submittal checklist: [Administrative Home Occupation Application](https://www.cityofboise.org/media/17933/202411-administrative-home-occupation-checklist.pdf). Do not assume a home-occupation permit legalizes a walk-up oatmeal restaurant in a residential driveway.

Parks and Greenbelt: (208) 608-7600, from the City vendor page.

Meridian, Garden City, Eagle, and Nampa are not Boise. Use that city's clerk and zoning if the site is there.

## 10. Certified food protection manager and training

Idaho requires food establishments to have at least one Certified Food Protection Manager. Accredited providers are listed through the ANSI National Accreditation Board, and CDH offers ServSafe. The Idaho Food Safety Exam is a separate, more basic certificate and does not meet the CFPM requirement. Whether a one-day TFE operator must hold a CFPM, or only complete the Temporary Food Establishment exam, is a question for CDH. Sources: DHW food safety page and CDH food establishments page, checked November 11, 2026. TFE exam and manual are linked from both sites.
