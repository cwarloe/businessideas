## Experiment 1: understand the purchase

Talk with 8–12 likely buyers across personal gifting, office buying and everyday treats. Ask:

1. When did you last buy fudge or a similar gift? What did you buy, where and for how much?
2. Was it for yourself, sharing or a gift? Why that product instead of cookies or chocolates?
3. What made ordering, packaging or pickup inconvenient?
4. What would you compare an 8 oz fudge box with?
5. Once a specific offer is available: would you actually purchase it at the stated price and pickup time?

Record actual behavior separately from compliments. Friends can provide recipe feedback, but friendly enthusiasm is weak evidence of repeat demand. Do not treat a hypothetical “yes” as an order.

## Experiment 2: compare product and occasion

After resolving production, labels, sampling and sale requirements, test repeatable recipes and show two clearly specified options: a simple take-home box and a gift-ready flight. Compare equivalent weights, track packaging differences, and avoid changing recipe, weight, price and delivery all at once.

Measure texture preference, perceived sweetness, willingness to pay and the occasion. Include non-family testers. Record allergies before offering samples and provide accurate ingredient information.

## Experiment 3: a small paid batch

**Proposed ceiling:** 20 × 8 oz boxes, one production session and one handoff window. Set a cash-risk limit and total-hour target before buying supplies. Consider a smaller batch if those limits require it. No orders are being taken by this repository.

Illustrative decision gates, to agree before the experiment:

- At least 15 boxes committed at the real stated price before making the planned batch; exclude free samples and discounts from full-price conversion.
- Log ingredient and package spend, saleable yield, rejects, refunds and every owner hour.
- Compare owner-hour contribution with a chosen target; the economics example uses $30/hour only as a placeholder.
- Follow up within 30–45 days for actual second purchases or referrals that become purchases. Holiday demand alone does not validate a weekly business.
- Repeat with a different group before scaling. A single good batch is encouraging, not a durable sales forecast.

If taste feedback is strong but purchases are weak, change occasion, offer or channel before expanding the flavor menu. If sales are strong but owner-hour contribution is weak, simplify packing/handoff, retest pricing or stop. If a production partner is attractive, model its actual quote and requirements rather than assuming the homemade margins apply.
