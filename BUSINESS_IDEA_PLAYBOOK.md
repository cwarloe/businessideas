# Business Idea Playbook — from curiosity to an evidence-based pilot

**Version:** 2026-11-18 · **Owner:** Charles Warloe · **Purpose:** Reuse what worked (and what failed) in the Oats, Etc. exploration for food, physical products, services, and software.

This is a **process**, not a claim that any concept has been market-validated. The record of a particular idea belongs in that idea's folder. Start with the [new-idea template](playbook/new-idea-template.md), read the [Oats lessons](playbook/lessons-learned.md), and review the [portfolio inventory](playbook/archive-status-2026-11-18.md).

## Five operating principles

1. **Find a customer and a reason they would buy.** Look for a recurring problem, a convenience or a purchase occasion you can reach. Mockups help describe the offer. Paid behavior tests it.
2. **Check economics early.** A market gap is not necessarily a profitable gap. Count owner's time, spoilage, capital, insurance, rent, payment fees, and working capital. Separate *revenue*, *contribution*, *operating profit*, and *return per owner-hour*.
3. **Sequence decisions by the cheapest way to disprove them.** Validate customer, channel, legal/operational feasibility and willingness to pay before costly branding, infrastructure or equipment.
4. **Keep hypotheses and evidence apart.** A survey, casual compliment, published competitor price, verbal host interest, paid preorder and repeat purchase are not interchangeable.
5. **Preserve negative knowledge.** When a route fails or is abandoned, log why, with source, date and reversal condition. Avoid solving the same problem twice.

## Quick-start: the next business idea in one sitting

1. Copy [new-idea-template.md](playbook/new-idea-template.md) into `ideas/<idea-slug>/README.md`.
2. In 15 minutes, state the buyer’s job or purchase occasion, sketch two or three offers and set limits on time, cash and risk. Mark assumptions as **HYPOTHESIS**.
3. In 30 minutes, compare 5–10 actual alternatives (including "do nothing"). Use linked primary sources and customer quotes where possible. State gaps in evidence.
4. In 15 minutes, write a one-page unit economics model and choose **one** cheap demand experiment. Do not purchase infrastructure yet.
5. Add a dated decision-log entry and source register. Save all work before switching chats, models, or agents.

## Start with the customer’s options

Write a short version of each credible offer before choosing which one to model. For each, record **who pays, what they want in that moment, what they do today, where they can buy, why they would switch and what would bring them back**. Use occasion or preference as well as pain. A treat, convenience or a habit can motivate a purchase. Let the founder choose the limits on cash, time and complexity. Where the founder has no preference, let customer behavior and operating results settle the choice.

| Question | Milk example | What would move the decision |
|---|---|---|
| What would they buy for later? | A take-home bottle ordered in advance and collected at a regular stop. | Paid pickups and repeat orders at the proposed price. |
| What would they buy now? | An individual cup of cold or warm milk at a stand. | Paid cups sold per service hour and purchases across more than one visit. |
| What would they pay to have delivered? | Several preordered bottles delivered to one office or neighboring households. | Paid orders clustered closely enough to cover the operator’s whole route. |

A new product or channel can change the test. The Oats office model quantified what cocoa could add to a breakfast order. It left open why someone would buy milk on its own. Treat an add-on and a standalone offer as separate hypotheses. When testing two offers at the same stop, use comparable exposure where practical and record who bought each, what they paid and what they later reordered. Document any differences in time, audience or exposure. These are examples for designing a test. The Oats and Milk Bar records remain desk research. ## Turn the research into a working business plan

Start with the [idea template](playbook/new-idea-template.md). After the market and economics pass, copy the [business plan template](playbook/business-plan-template.md) into the idea folder. Use it to make the next investment decision. For each major claim, connect the buyer and offer to the market evidence, how you will reach and serve them, the economics, and the next test. Keep links to detailed research and the cost model so you can update the plan without recopying them.

Before a research round, name one decision the work should unlock and the two or three findings that could change it. Start with a quick screen of real alternatives, prices, suppliers and likely constraints. Deepen the research where a wrong assumption would change the decision. Every round should end with the current best offer, what would overturn it, and the smallest next test with an owner, budget and decision date. Make the choice to proceed, revise, pause or stop explicit. A short plan can govern a small test. A larger investment needs more evidence and a more detailed plan.

### Prompt to start the next idea

> Use this playbook and the new-idea template to develop [idea] for [initial market]. Propose two or three offers for specific buyers and purchase occasions. Compare what buyers do today, who pays, where they can be reached, and what would bring them back. Use dated primary evidence for material facts and preserve the strongest negative findings. Compare the cost and time of the simplest delivery formats with a base and downside case. Identify the assumption most likely to change the decision, and design one small test with a price, audience, budget, pass/fail threshold and decision date. Fill in the working business plan, preserve open decisions and link the evidence. Use the approved scope for research, outreach and spending.

## Stages and evidence gates

| Stage | Main question | Minimum deliverable | Move forward when… |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 · Intake | Does this fit Charles's goals, time, money and abilities? | One-page idea brief; target customer; hard constraints | Clear customer and feasible time/capital ceiling |
| 1 · Problem | What job, preference or occasion drives the purchase? | Customer/problem statement, current alternatives, interview plan | Some first-hand evidence of a meaningful reason to buy |
| 2 · Market & channel | Who buys, where can they be reached, why now? | Competitor/substitute table, local/national scope, reachable customer channel, sources | A plausible path to a *specific* group of buyers |
| 3 · Economics | Can it earn enough to justify effort and risk? | Price, variable cost, margin, fixed cost, capex, downside sensitivity, owner-hours | A plausible profitable scenario **and** a cheap way to test it; no invented price/cost confidence |
| 4 · Feasibility | Can it legally and practically be delivered? | Regulatory/IP checklist, operational bottlenecks, required approvals and dependencies | No unresolved *fatal* gate before taking the next proposed action |
| 5 · Demand test | Will people actually pay? | Test design, sample, price, success/fail threshold, recorded observations | Customer behaviors meet a **pre-registered** threshold—not just positive reactions |
| 6 · Pilot | Can the promised result be delivered repeatedly? | Small compliant pilot, actual labor/throughput/waste/retention and P&L | Real performance supports continuation, adjustment, or a pivot |
| 7 · Build/scale | Which investments now remove a proven constraint? | Prioritized build plan, updated risk register, postmortem | Evidence justifies spending; prior tests and lessons are archived |

**The stages may loop.** A legal or trademark red flag can arise at intake; a rough financial screen belongs near the beginning, not after months of design. "Not yet verified" is a valid gate outcome.

### Profit screen for every idea

Compare candidates on these dimensions before committing significant time:

- **Demand:** pain intensity, buying frequency, verified behavior, reachability.
- **Contribution:** selling price minus materials, packaging, fulfillment, transaction fees, refunds/spoilage and other per-sale costs.
- **Overhead and owner labor:** kitchen or space rental, permits, insurance, subscriptions, utilities, marketing, commuting, prep, teardown, administration. Show both *cash profit* and profit after an explicit owner-time value.
- **Capital and downside:** startup outlay, required inventory, equipment resale value, recurring commitments, worst-case loss.
- **Feasibility:** skills, permissions, safety, regulation, supplier dependency, seasonal or peak-hour capacity.
- **Scalability:** repeat purchase, fulfillment repeatability, ability to serve a second customer without doubling Charles's hours.

A simple weighted score can help compare candidates, but **do not treat a numerical score as evidence**. Disqualify an option when a hard constraint fails.

## Demand evidence ladder

| Signal | What it actually establishes |
|---|---|
| AI brainstorm / desk research | Candidate hypotheses, **no buyer validation** |
| Public likes, votes or free survey | Directional preference in a biased sample; not a sales forecast |
| Interview with a defined buyer | Articulated pain and alternatives; stated intent may exaggerate demand |
| Qualified inquiry / host agreement | Reachability and channel access; not repeat purchase |
| Paid preorder or genuine paid pilot | Stronger price and demand evidence at that moment |
| Repeated purchases with positive actual margin | Much stronger operating validation |

For each experiment record the denominator, segment, actual price, date, participants, behavior and failure threshold. Distinguish repeat customers from one-time novelty sales.

## Research and review method

1. **Define falsifiable questions.** Example: "Can a particular Boise office location generate enough paid breakfast orders more than once?" rather than "Would a breakfast cart be fun?"
2. **Use primary sources** for laws, fees, marks, official market data and product specifications; add dated citations and note what was *not* checked. Customer interviews and pilot receipts are first-hand evidence.
3. **Compare alternatives**, including a cheaper business model that removes equipment, real estate, inventory or compliance complexity.
4. **Ask for an adversarial pass:** the strongest reason this will fail; hidden costs; invalid inference; cheaper experiment; criteria to abandon.
5. **Run a separate correction pass** against the actual source documents. Record errors as corrections instead of rewriting history.
6. **Update the decision log**: adopted, rejected, deferred, open; who decided; why; what evidence would reverse it.

## Reusable prompts

### Opportunity search

> Identify underserved customer jobs in [market/geography] for [customer type]. Include substitutes and competing providers, direct customer complaint/demand signals, possible distribution channels and evidence with dates/links. Score *potential* unit contribution, startup capital, recurring overhead, labor intensity, regulatory barriers, fulfillment complexity, repeat purchase, and downside risk. Flag hypotheses vs observed evidence. Avoid recommending a "gap" simply because few competitors exist. Shortlist the three cheapest credible real-world tests and list findings requiring verification.

### Adversarial business review

> Act as a skeptical buyer, operations manager and financial reviewer. Review the current idea brief, source register, cost model and test design. Identify the ten most consequential unproven assumptions and what would make the business unprofitable or impractical. For each, propose the cheapest decisive test, evidence threshold, and reversal condition. Do not silently replace earlier decisions; write corrections and open questions.

### Brand/offer comparison (only when useful)

> Develop three genuinely distinct positioning/visual directions for the *same verified customer problem* and delivery constraints. Specify target customer, urgent benefit, differentiation, headline, calls to action and risks. Check likely naming conflicts using official and common-law sources; label preliminary screening, not legal clearance. Do not build three production stacks to test three headlines. Capture feedback separately from actual purchase behavior.

### Session handoff / archive

> Before ending, update this idea's README, dated decision log, source register, assumptions, experiments, unresolved questions, and lessons learned. Cite file paths, commits/PRs and external primary sources. Separate proposed, built, deployed and field-verified. List exactly what remains unpublished or on an unmerged branch. Do not claim a chat transcript or attachment is archived unless it was actually copied into the repository.

## GitHub and deployment discipline

- Branch from current `main`; use a descriptive `research/` or `docs/` branch. Follow [CONTRIBUTING.md](CONTRIBUTING.md) and open a reviewable PR. A file on an open PR **is not on `main`**.
- Each idea has a concise index, source/assumptions log, decision log, cheap tests, financials, competing alternatives, technical assets, and a dated retrospective. Keep old rejected options, labeled rejected.
- GitHub Pages prototype = visual demonstration, **not** payment demand proof. Enable under **Settings → Pages → Deploy from branch → main /docs** only for repos using that layout. Check the deployed URL and cache/version after each change.
- Cloudflare Workers/D1 are optional when an idea needs server persistence, voting or API functionality. Keep project-specific databases/Workers separate, never commit credentials, and test published endpoints. Poll counts from an anonymous browser identifier are **not unique humans**.
- Publish a change only when the merge/deployment is verified. Record live URL, branch, commit, date, checks run and any blockers.
- Retain citations and dates for changing legal, market, pricing and platform details; reverify before spending or launching.
- Do not put private customer data, secret keys, personal contact lists, or full copyrighted documents in a public repo.

## Definition of "archived"

**Archived** means: every major idea, rejected option, pivot rationale, sourced finding, material mistake, artifact link, outstanding unknown, and current decision is represented in a file that has been **merged to `main`**; all needed external assets are linked or included, and the index points to them. An open PR, private site, unrecorded chat, inaccessible attachment, or hypothetical research finding does **not** satisfy that standard.

A good archive makes the next decision faster and prevents repeating a mistake. It need not reproduce every conversational sentence verbatim.
